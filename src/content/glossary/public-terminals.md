Public terminals, once designed as digital commons for accessing civic services, are now biometriccontrolled interfaces that exclude the undocumented and un-scored. In 2075, these terminals are ubiquitous—found in train stations, post offices, food distribution points, and libraries. Their screens offer state-approved news, weather forecasts, ration updates, and surveillance alerts. But for Sumati, they function as walls, not windows: she cannot log in, request help, or confirm her identity. Her father’s disappearance is not recorded because she cannot access the system. The novel's terminals are a recurring motif, representing how the state's architecture no longer recognises people outside its scoring logic. Designed during the IKS “tech-nationalism” boom, they were introduced with grand rhetoric about access and equity. Over time, they became tools for quiet erasure. In real life, parallels exist in Aadhaarauthenticated services, where access is contingent on fingerprint scans or OTPs, often failing at crucial moments. In the story, public terminals are not broken—they are perfectly functional, which is the problem. Their very design ensures that those most in need will likely be locked out.
