The digital display chart is a legacy information system still in use at smaller or underfunded railway stations. It is an electronic noticeboard that shows train schedules, platform allocations and delay warnings. Unlike the dynamic holographic interfaces at major transit hubs, these displays are LED or e-ink based, often decades old, and reliant on intermittent power. They were once a symbol of modernisation in the early 2040s, and by 2075 they had become markers of neglect and infrastructure decay.

The one in Nandini’s tea stall is typical: dusty, glitch-prone and patched with local workarounds to sync with the central rail server. Despite their outdated form, these charts remain vital for travellers, especially in Inadaptable Zones where AI assistants are unreliable, mobile devices are locked to biometric profiles and satcomms do not connect to public networks. When Mrs Sharma checks the chart for the next train to Kolkata, it works as a last tether to the national transport grid.

In a world where connectivity is fragmented and permissions are policed, the digital display chart still works without asking who you are. Its continued use reflects systemic inequality and the quiet persistence of analogue infrastructure.
