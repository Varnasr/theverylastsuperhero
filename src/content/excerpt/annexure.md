*Why cities drown and children burn: the climate science behind The Very Last Superhero*

By 2075, India will be hotter, wetter and more unequal. The heat, the drowned neighbourhoods and the cooling uniforms in this novel are drawn from published research, chiefly a 2024 systematic review of heatwave projections for India (Ravindra et al., *Heliyon*) and a 2025 study of how cities around the world recover from floods (Gandhi et al., *Journal of Public Economics*).

### 1. Heat and human limits

Heat already kills in India. The 2024 review counts more than 24,000 deaths from heatwaves between 1992 and 2015. In 2015 and 2019, daily maximum temperatures stayed above 40°C for a month or more, and for around 45 days in some places.

The projections are worse. One study in the review finds that severe heatwaves in India become eight times as frequent by 2021–2050 even under a low-warming scenario, and up to forty times as frequent by 2100 under high emissions. For Delhi and other cities, it reports a rise of 1.5°C in mean maximum temperature since the 1980s and a further 1.5°C by 2050 under high emissions.

Children like Sumati need heat-regulating school uniforms. Cooling suits were once issued to soldiers and relief workers, and in the novel they have become a middle-class necessity. Access to them is unequal. Survival in the book depends on whether a family can afford to adapt.

### 2. Drowning cities and unequal recovery

The 2025 study assembles every major flood in 9,468 cities across 175 countries between 2000 and 2023, and measures economic activity by the lights a city gives off at night. On average, a flood dims a city's night lights by about 3 percent. In cities in low-income countries the fall is 3.5 times larger than in cities in high-income countries, and recovery is slower.

In the novel, Kishanganj is one such "inadaptable" zone, marked by aquifer collapse, broken ration supply and institutions that have stopped working. Its condition follows the pattern the study describes, where the poorest places take the largest losses and recover last.

The study also finds that cities with more experience of flooding suffer smaller losses, and that flood damage has fallen over time. Cities do adapt. Wealthier ones adapt faster, while peripheral towns like Ramnagar are left behind.

### 3. Migration is not a safety net

Leaving is one way to adapt. The study finds that migration makes cities more resilient to floods in high-income countries and does much less in low-income ones, where cities that flood again and again keep growing. People stay, because money, family and policy hold them in place.

Sumati's parents stay too. They rewire their home, ration energy and adapt a little at a time. They have very little choice in it, and the novel treats what looks like resilience as a trap.

### 4. When memory becomes infrastructure

In the novel, crystalline salt lamps act as data beacons that conduct, sense and store. The science is speculative, though conductive minerals and new memory materials are under active study.

The lamps stand against the failure of real infrastructure. The 2025 study finds that dams protect cities from flood damage in high-income countries and protect them far less in low-income ones. The novel's Treasure Room keeps the memory that the state failed to record.

### 5. Adaptation is unequal, and still possible

Adaptation is already happening, and it depends on money, memory and continuity. Cooling suits, AI bureaucracies, rationed electricity and closed train stations are adaptations already underway.

*This annexure describes the present. If it reads as exaggerated, that is because the measurements have not yet caught up with the next emergency.*
