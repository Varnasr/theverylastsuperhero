About

What we choose to remember

The Very Last Superhero is a fully illustrated speculative fiction novel set in a climate-collapsed India, blending AI, memory and resistance through the eyes of a fourteen-year-old girl.

It begins after the era of superheroes rather than during it. In the 2040s and 50s, state- and corporate-backed heroes publicly tackled crime, pollution and dissent, and surveillance — welcomed at first — became ordinary. What followed was not a villain but a sequence: the Bangladesh flood, the Disclosures, an AI-authored National Curriculum, and the failure of four embankments on the Yamuna.

In the ruins, heroism is not superpowers or spectacle. It is memory, care, and the courage to hold a community together when everything else has fallen apart. The Resistance is not an army. It is librarians, scientists, elders and teenagers running a filing system nobody in power can find.

Themes

  • Climate collapse and adaptation
  • Memory, oral history and the archive
  • AI, surveillance and technology ethics
  • Youth agency and intergenerational knowledge
  • Care economies and post-heroic politics

Influences

Literary: Parable of the Sower by Octavia Butler, the Broken Earth trilogy by N. K. Jemisin, Delhi: A Novel by Khushwant Singh, and The Ministry for the Future by Kim Stanley Robinson.

Visual: South Asian street photography, post-flood ruins, faded signage and handwritten notes, Diwali lights against decay. Narrative: real oral-history archives, resistance zines, and Indigenous mapmaking traditions.

On the making of it

The story emerged from a question about what happens after the era of superheroes — in a world where memory, oral traditions and resilience outlast spectacle. The Salt Lamp system was drawn from Himalayan salt's conductivity and its folklore. Centring a young protagonist amid ruins was a response to how we imagine intergenerational knowledge in collapsed states.

Recurring motifs: birds, broken infrastructure, care, fragments, surveillance decay.

Collapse doesn't mean the end. It means the truth has outlived its suppressors.

— Rohan Kapoor, encrypted Salt Lamp log

I don't want a hero. I want a neighbour.

— Sumati

Why waste-to-energy

The novel is dedicated in part to Dev, "for being the courageous father he is, battling Delhi's waste-to-energy plants all along." That fight is not fictional, and the book's argument — that the unglamorous, community-based work is the work that counts — comes directly out of it.

You can read about the ongoing campaign at The battle against Delhi's waste-to-energy plants.
